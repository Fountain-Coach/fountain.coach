Burning down the house

Memory work starts the way a stage starts: with a clean room, a small set, objects placed as if they knew what they were doing. A bed that says nothing, a table that pretends to be ordinary, two chairs that wait without insisting. The light arrives too early, a hard beam, like morning that doesn’t ask whether I’m ready. I stand outside the room-within-room and call it “my life” because I need a frame. I call it “home” because I need somewhere to put my hands.

At first it is almost calming: the idea that memory can be furnished. That something so unruly could be given edges. The bed as prop, the table as ritual, the chairs as the possibility of two people sitting down without it becoming a negotiation. If I keep it minimal, maybe it won’t break. If I name it clearly, maybe it stays.

But the second panel is always waiting. The seam doesn’t open because I force it. It opens because it exists. A crack in the back wall, paper tearing along the line it was already planning to tear. The set holds, but now the set is also confession. Behind the wallpaper: rough structure, old drafts, scratched-out tries, the scaffolding that was never meant to be seen. I tell myself it’s only staging, only material, only a repair attempt. Still: the room changes when the seam is visible. The air changes. The promise of calm becomes a question.

I know this pressure vector. I can draw it. I can label it. It rises straight up from the floor like a spear of meaning: something in me insists on explaining. On finding the reason. On turning every small object into evidence. Over-lit, over-meaning, cold. I’ve lived long enough with this reflex to recognize its elegance and its violence. It makes a room legible while it makes it unlivable.

In the third panel, the witnesses arrive. Soft figures. Not judges. Not rescuers. They stand on the edge of the tear the way animals stand at the edge of a clearing: careful, quiet, listening with their whole bodies. They don’t ask for a story. They don’t demand a conclusion. They simply confirm that something is happening. There is an eye symbol floating in the upper air: witness mode. The gentlest instruction I know. Look. Don’t rush. Don’t grab.

And then the smallest thing: a dot of orange, like a seed of fire. Not the big fire—never the big fire. Not catastrophe. Not punishment. Not the flames that turn a childhood day into a lifelong weather system. This is the small fire I can protect. The orange heart that doesn’t need to justify itself with philosophy. A warmth that fits in cupped hands. A warmth that can exist even when the wall is torn and the set is exposed.

Distance and nearness are not moral categories. They are a meter on the page. A sliding marker that moves with the day, with the body, with sleep, with winter air. I used to think nearness had to be won by making everything intelligible. I used to think love required a perfect explanation, a coherent narrative, the right title. Now I’m learning another rule: the seam can be visible and the room can still be inhabited. The staging can be exposed and I can still choose tenderness. I can still place a chair. I can still set a small table. I can still keep the orange dot alive.

Memory begins as a set before it becomes a story. That means I don’t have to write the whole story tonight. I only have to return to the room. I only have to notice where the pressure is rising. I only have to enter witness mode for a moment and let the soft figures stand where they stand. And if I can do that, if I can do only that, then even in winter, even with seams and latency and no reply, something in me remembers how to stay warm without burning down the house.
