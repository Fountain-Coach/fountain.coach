Bee's Journal ( " Love , Grounding, Movement " )

I notice how quickly form arrives, even when I am trying to stay with something that has not yet taken shape. It is almost immediate: the moment an image appears, it begins to settle, to declare itself as something, to suggest meaning, relation, intention. Even here, with these fragments—the flowering branch, the tagged plant, the exposed fish in the green plastic—the same movement begins. They do not remain what they are for long. They start to organize into a statement, into a composition that asks to be read.

And I see that I participate in that. I allow it, sometimes I even accelerate it, because it gives me ground. The empty space of the room does not remain empty for long; it becomes a stage, then an installation, then a position. The elements begin to speak too loudly, too clearly. The branch becomes care, the fish becomes abandonment or waste, the distance between them becomes a line that can be interpreted. It stabilizes the field, but it also closes it.

What I am trying to understand is what happens if I do not follow that movement all the way. If I let the objects remain slightly out of reach of their own meaning. Not withholding interpretation as a strategy, but simply not completing it.

Because what I notice in these attempts—the renderings, the placements—is that they arrive too quickly at a kind of finality. Even when they are provisional, they behave as if they are resolved. They carry a quiet claim: this is what it is. And something in me resists that, not as critique, but as a kind of fatigue. It feels like an imposition on something that has not yet finished appearing.

The branch is still a branch. It carries the tag, yes, but the tag does not stabilize it. It moves slightly, it hangs, it is contingent. The fish is not yet an image. It is too present, too exposed, not integrated into anything that would make it readable. It interrupts.

And the space between them is not yet a relation. It is only distance.

If I stay there, without assembling them into a system, something else becomes perceptible. The space begins to function differently. It is not a neutral container, and not yet a structured field. It is more like a condition that allows these things to remain unaligned.

This is close to what I was trying to describe with non-integrational love, though I see now how easily that, too, becomes a concept that organizes what it touches. I am not certain of the term. I only recognize the movement: not bringing things into coherence too early.

Because the moment coherence arrives, grounding also appears—but it is a specific kind of grounding. It depends on the stability of the arrangement. It reassures by fixing relations.

Without that, grounding has to come from somewhere else.

In this space, where the elements do not yet hold together, I cannot rely on their relation to orient myself. I have to remain with my own perception, my own contact with what is there, even if it does not form a whole.

This is less comfortable. It does not offer the same clarity. But it feels more precise.

I begin to think that grounding, in this sense, is not the result of a stable structure, but the capacity to remain present in a field that has not yet resolved. To not rush toward meaning in order to feel located.

And love, if I bring it here carefully, seems to behave in a similar way. It does not need to become a system in order to exist. It does not require immediate confirmation, nor does it need to organize itself into a continuous relation. It can appear, intensify, withdraw, without stabilizing.

But this also means it cannot be secured by form.

So what remains is not a finished installation, not a clear composition, but something more like an influx—elements entering, touching, not fully connecting, not disappearing either. And my task, if I can call it that, is not to finalize them, but to stay with their movement without forcing them into place.
