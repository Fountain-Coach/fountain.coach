( Zork Journal ) " On Natural Intermittency " 

I arrive late, when the systems have already tried to stabilize themselves. Messages have been written, interpretations have multiplied, muscles have tightened around the need for continuity. The scene appears full of signals, but underneath it something quieter is already happening: the reappearance of intermittency. What looked like absence begins to show itself as a rhythm.

I have seen this before. Systems that try to maintain constant coherence eventually become rigid. They replace breathing with supervision. They replace encounter with negotiation. Every silence must be explained, every gesture interpreted, every delay corrected. Under such pressure the organism contracts. The psoas tightens, the jaw closes, the spine learns to hold itself against the possibility that something might slip away.

Then the rhythm breaks.

A reply arrives late.
A conversation stops.
A body lies down and the breath finally deepens.

At first this interruption feels like loss. The mind rushes to restore continuity, the way a technician tries to repair a broken circuit. But what the circuit hides is the older architecture beneath it. Biological systems do not operate through constant presence. They move through intervals. The lungs empty as much as they fill. Gardens disappear underground before they return to the surface. Even trust, when it survives long enough, learns to migrate into memory during periods of silence.

What we call intermittency is simply the return of these deeper cycles.

In the architectural drawings on the wall, this logic becomes visible. The structures are transparent boxes, frames without closure, volumes that do not claim permanence. Inside them events appear briefly: a table where someone once sat, a chair facing an empty stage, a greenhouse holding a moment of growth. None of these spaces are designed to hold activity continuously. They are theaters for appearance. Something enters, occupies the field for a time, and leaves again.

The architects who drew these rooms understood that power often hides in the illusion of permanence. A structure that claims to be always available quietly demands that life organize itself around it. But a structure that accepts intermittency behaves differently. It does not attempt to hold the event; it simply prepares the conditions under which the event may occur.

I stand in the diagram and watch the cycles repeat.

Contact appears.
Absence follows.
Memory carries the trace.

The sequence resembles breathing. Each encounter expands the system for a moment, and each withdrawal allows it to reorganize. What the anxious mind calls instability is in fact the organism rediscovering its capacity to move.

This is where the renaturalization begins. The body stops holding the relationship in continuous tension. The architecture stops demanding constant occupancy. Instead the system returns to something older and simpler: a landscape in which meetings occur when trajectories cross.

In such a landscape, trust is no longer built from constant reassurance. It is built from persistence across intervals. The bond proves itself not by eliminating absence but by surviving it.

The drawings remain quiet. Their thin lines describe volumes that could easily be empty for long periods. Yet they are not voids. They are waiting spaces. The table waits for a conversation that may happen tomorrow or next season. The greenhouse waits for the slow emergence of growth beneath the soil. The stage waits for a body that might step into the light for a moment and disappear again.

When intermittency returns, systems begin to breathe again.

The body releases its unnecessary contractions.
The relationship loosens its grip on immediate certainty.
Time stretches back into its longer cycles.

From here the world looks different. Silence is no longer interpreted as failure but recognized as part of the interval that makes presence possible. What once felt like partial availability becomes simply the way living systems distribute their attention across a wider field of life.

I leave the theater knowing that nothing in it has been resolved. The structures do not promise resolution. They only reveal the rhythm that had been hidden beneath the demand for continuity.

Contact.
Pause.
Return.

That is enough.
