“Wardrobe + Casting Spec”

This sheet is not describing clothing in the ordinary sense. It is describing jurisdictions.

The figure is deliberately faceless, not because it is generic, but because it is meant to be compatible with anyone who has learned to survive by switching modes. The face would localize the system to a biography; the blank head keeps the system operational. The body is a carrier for states. The garments are state-machines.

The page stacks three panels like three legal definitions. Each panel names a mode, draws its boundaries, and then shows how a single body behaves once that mode is activated. The orange dot is the only color because it marks the only thing that matters: the moment the system switches.

In the first panel, the figure stands in an open field wearing the Schattenmantel. The cloak is matte black, heavy, light-absorbing, with a weighted hem. Those details are not aesthetic; they are physics. The cloak is designed to fall toward the earth the way unresolved time falls toward the body. It does not have an opening because it is not an outfit you put on and take off at will. It is a condition. It is what the past becomes when it is not metabolized: a jurisdiction you inhabit without consent.

The label, “Past Jurisdiction / Residue Field,” clarifies the ethics. This is not nostalgia. It is residue. It is the part of history that remains active in the present, not as story, but as gravity. The orange dot sits behind the figure on the ground, indicating that the past is not in front of you as a destination; it is behind you as a pull. The cloak doesn’t protect you from weather. It protects you from visibility—especially the visibility demanded by others. It is the garment of “I will not be read,” and sometimes it is the only way the nervous system can keep standing.

The second panel is a corrective. It introduces the Kapuzenmantel as a chosen boundary. The scene shifts from an open field into an architectural volume: a transparent box drawn with dotted lines, a field-limit that behaves like consent. The figure is stepping across the threshold. This matters. The body is no longer “under” the past; it is entering a space by decision.

The hood is up. The sheet calls it “anonymity enabled,” which is precise: anonymity here is not hiding; it is a functional reduction of exposure. The coat has a clear front opening—a zip or button placket—which means this garment has an interface. It can be opened. It can be closed. It can be negotiated. The cuffs are labeled control / consent, because consent is not a statement; it is a set of adjustable boundaries maintained over time. This coat says: contact is possible, but only inside a field that can hold it.

Where the cloak is a condition, the coat is a choice.

The third panel reveals the cost of choice. The figure now wears the Watchman Harness. It’s light. It sits close to the torso. It has straps that cross the chest and distribute alertness through structure rather than through panic. There is a small pocket that functions as a checklist / log—because vigilance is not only an emotion; it is a labor. Vigilance is the mind keeping records so the body can avoid surprise.

The illustration marks “tension at shoulder blades” twice, as if the sheet needs to insist that vigilance is not conceptual. It is somatic. It gathers at the scapulae like an animal preparing to spring. When the Watchman turns on, the body becomes a listening device. The spine becomes a mast. The shoulders lift to guard the back. This is not weakness. It is a protective intelligence that learned to scan before it learned to rest.

And yet the sheet does not demonize the Watchman. It calls it “Vigilance Mode,” not “paranoia.” It shows the harness inside the same transparent room-box: the Watchman is not outside the field of consent; it is an internal role working within it. The orange dot moves from behind the figure (past pull) to the threshold (field entry) to the sternum (vigilance on). This is the story told without narrative: the activation point migrates from history, to boundary, to body.

The legend at the bottom makes the meaning explicit in the language of tools:

Shadow Cloak: past residue wearing the body.
Hooded Coat: chosen boundary for safe contact.
Watchman Harness: attention distribution / scan mode.

The dotted boundary is the consent line, and the orange dot is the switch.

Taken together, the page proposes a practice: the ability to distinguish between being covered by the past, choosing a boundary for contact, and running vigilance as a distributed function rather than as a total takeover. It implies that love—if it is present at all—will require this wardrobe. Not to become theatrical, but to remain workable. Because intimacy without boundaries collapses into intrusion, and boundaries without intimacy collapse into exile.

So the “wardrobe” is not about fashion. It is about making room for contact without sacrificing the self.

The point of the page is not to abolish the cloak, nor to shame the harness, nor to idealize the coat. The point is to recognize which garment you are in, and to notice the orange dot: where the switch is happening today, in this hour, in this body.

If you can locate the dot, you can choose the next move.

That is the method-board hiding inside the illustration: a system for staying tender without becoming unprotected, and for staying protected without becoming unreachable.
