" The Raft "

I enter a room that isn’t a room. It’s a blank, technical space where objects stand alone, as if they have been extracted from life and placed on a drafting table.

A bed sits there—too clean, too isolated, too public. I register it as “place of sleep,” but also as “place of witness.” There’s no wall to protect it. Everything that happens here happens in the open.

I’m holding papers. I’m reading, sorting, rewriting. The phrase “TEXT CHANGED” hovers like a stamp on the scene, and I feel its weight: something in the story has been revised without my consent, or perhaps by a part of me that finally took over. Pages fall to the floor. The falling isn’t dramatic—it’s administrative. It’s the sound of an old narrative being dropped because it can’t be carried any longer.

When I look up, I’m suddenly not alone. People are present, seated in a row like quiet witnesses. Their posture is calm, almost formal. In front of them the bed is no longer only a bed. Bodies lie piled on it—close, layered, heavy with warmth and history. The closeness doesn’t feel erotic. It feels like a human attempt to create continuity through contact, like a family trying to keep something alive by staying physically together.

I step toward them and I speak—simple, direct, like an instruction that has been waiting behind my throat:

“Let us remember our mother.”

The sentence has a strange authority. It isn’t sentimental. It isn’t an explanation. It’s a ritual command: not to “talk about,” but to “call in.” I feel that the word mother is not just a person here—it’s a center of gravity. Saying it changes the temperature of the scene.

I notice an orange dot on my back, printed on my shirt like a target or an ember. It’s small but undeniable. It marks something in me that stays lit. A duty-point. A signal that I am the one who remains responsible, even when the ground shifts.

The bodies on the bed rearrange. The witnesses fade. The crowd becomes less necessary. The scene simplifies until only the essential structure remains: one body lying down, one body sitting up.

And then the physics change.

The bed is floating on water.

The water is not violent. It’s calm, almost diagrammatic—horizontal lines, a quiet surface. The bed becomes a raft. A platform that can carry weight without certainty. I sit at the edge of it, upright, facing outward, while another body rests behind me. I feel myself as watchful—not tense, not heroic, simply present. I’m there in the way someone stays awake so someone else can sleep.

In this final configuration, the ritual has done its work. The mother is still not visible, but remembering her has reorganized everything. The papers and the “text changed” moment lead here: to a stripped-down scene where I don’t fix the situation, I don’t solve it, I don’t explain it. I keep the raft steady.

I feel the meaning in my posture: I do not abandon what is vulnerable in me. I do not abandon the place where remembering happens. I sit at the edge of the bed-boat and let the water be what it is.

The dream leaves me with a quiet instruction that isn’t spoken: when the ground disappears, I can still carry. Not by force, but by staying.
