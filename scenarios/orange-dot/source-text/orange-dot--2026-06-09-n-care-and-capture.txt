( N. Journal )

I don’t like how correct it is. That is the first thing. Not that it is too ugly. Not that it is too harsh. It is worse than ugly because it has been made properly. The joints fit. The frame holds. The desk is useful. The reclining part is useful. The shirt is held up. The sheet is ready. Everything has a reason, and that is where the brutality sits.

It looks like someone loved method more than the child. Or maybe that is too easy. Maybe it looks like someone believed method was the way to love the child. That is harder to bear. The object is not cruel in a theatrical way. It does not punish. It accommodates. It provides a place to sit, to write, to recline, to be supported, to be held upright when one cannot hold oneself. It is full of care, and still I feel trapped by it.

The purple shirt is the worst part because it is soft. If it were metal, I could defend myself. But it is a shirt. It has buttons, a collar, a pocket. It carries the promise of an ordinary body. It says: someone could wear this. Someone did wear this. Someone was expected to appear here and be recognized through procedure.

The body is gone, but the apparatus does not notice. It keeps the position open. It keeps the back straight. It keeps the table at the correct height. It keeps the sheet in front. The absence is not allowed to relax. Even absence has to sit properly. I feel that in my shoulders.

The grid-sheet is almost funny, but not funny enough. It has the look of invented writing, inherited writing, writing that someone taught as if it were freedom. Marks in rows. Rows in a system. A red block at the end, as if the exercise has reached a zone of special seriousness. I know that kind of page. It says: make your own language, but make it here. In this box. In this disciplined field. Be original inside the structure that proves you are original.

That is the parents’ inventionism, maybe. Not invention as escape. Invention as family law. I tried to be true to it. That sentence is already tired. I tried to be true to my parents’ inventionism. I hear the obedience in it. I hear the love too, and that makes it more difficult. The phrase wants to be generous. It wants to say: they gave me something. They gave me a way to make the world strange. They did not leave me with plain reality. They built procedures, images, objects, private rules, odd freedoms. But the child receives freedom as inheritance, and inheritance is heavy even when it is made of play.

The bench part frightens me because it means the desk already knows I will get tired. It has planned for my exhaustion. It has attached rest to work, but not as release. More as continuation. If you cannot sit, recline. If you cannot write upright, lie near the writing. If you cannot appear as a person, the shirt will appear. If you cannot complete the exercise, the apparatus will keep you inside it until completion becomes irrelevant.

I don’t want to call this abuse. That word is too loud and too clean. I don’t want to call it love either, because that would make the mechanism glow. It is something between care and capture. A designed tenderness that has forgotten the difference between holding and fixing in place.

What bothers me most is that the object is beautiful. Not decorative beautiful. Constructed beautiful. It has the calm of a thing that believes in itself. The wood, the metal, the small bolts, the measured supports. I can imagine someone being proud of having solved the problem. How do we hold the child in the procedure? Like this. How do we join study and fatigue? Like this. How do we keep the inherited language visible? Place the sheet here. How do we keep the body present? Hang the shirt.

And I am angry because part of me admires the solution. That is the brutality too: the child learns to admire the apparatus.

I don't want to spit on it.

I look at it and feel the old double movement. I want to crawl into it and I want to break it. I want to prove I can use it correctly and I want to refuse the whole premise. I want to say thank you for the invention and also: why did every invention require me to become compatible with it?

Maybe the object is not a portrait of a child. Maybe it is a portrait of fidelity after the child has left. The shirt remains because loyalty remains. The procedure remains because the family method remains. The reclining bench remains because the body remembers fatigue. The desk remains because there is still work to do. The sheet remains because the inherited signs have not been translated.

And I stand outside it now, which is already a kind of disobedience. I do not have to sit. I do not have to complete the red section. I do not have to be true by repeating the form. But I also do not want to spit on it. That would be too simple. The object is carrying something real: a failed gift, or a dangerous gift, or a gift that became too large for the hands of the one who received it.

I think the next drawing should not add anything. No horse. No classroom. No parents. No child. Only this impossible furniture. And maybe one small loosened screw. Not enough to destroy it. Enough to show that the body has begun to disagree.
