" Am Rand des Feldes
   zähl ich die Gefahren nicht mehr,
   nur was gilt.
   Mein kleines Herz Orange!
   Die ganze Stadt nehm ich ins Gepäck. " 

I wake up with the feeling that I have been moving through a sequence of rooms designed to teach me something simple: where my life begins, where it ends, and what it costs to keep stepping over the line.

The first image I keep is almost embarrassingly clean. A small house sits inside a fenced plot. No drama, no weather, no story—just a boundary drawn in the plainest possible way. I walk the perimeter like someone checking a lock, like someone measuring whether the edge is real. I don’t enter the house; I don’t decorate it; I don’t explain it. I circle it. I make sure the limits hold. I notice how my attention clings to the border rather than to the inside—how safety, for me, is often a question of containment before it becomes a question of comfort. The orange mark in my chest stays with me as I walk, like a small coal reminding me that the border is never abstract: it’s my body deciding what it can tolerate.

Then the dream shifts into a larger interior, and the tone changes. I am in a restoration lab—an improvised workshop where people sit close together around tables, tools, papers, devices. It feels communal, practical, almost tender in its focus. Things here are being repaired rather than judged. I’m not alone in the task. I’m part of an ecology of attention: hands, surfaces, shared concentration.

I stand near the front, holding a child. The child is quiet, heavy in the honest way children are—present, unquestioning, not interested in my concepts. With the other hand I greet a woman, and the gesture is ordinary: a handshake, a welcome, a recognition. It is the kind of contact that says, yes, you are real, and yes, you belong in the room where work is done. Behind us, others keep sorting, building, calibrating—like the world can be restored by steady labor rather than by intensity.

What strikes me is the door on the right: open, bright, unguarded. The room doesn’t feel like a trap. It feels like a place I can leave without punishment. I register something rare: structure without coercion. Help without capture. A collective without the old fear that I will be swallowed or exposed.

And then I am travelling.

The last panel is a return journey, and it carries the softness of aftercare. I sit on a train or subway with the city moving past the window—blocks, lines, the quiet geometry of distance. In my lap I hold a carton of eggs as if it were fragile information. I hold it the way I hold anything precious when I’m afraid of dropping it: close, careful, almost devotional. Next to me sits a small box—plain, closed, unnamed. I don’t open it in the image. I just keep it near, as if the point is not consumption but transport: I am bringing something back intact.

This is how the three scenes hang together for me.

First I define the boundary. Then I enter the lab where repair is possible. Then I travel home with what I managed to save.

The icons along the bottom feel like a quiet checklist I didn’t know I was writing: sensitivity, love, bed, weather, home. The dream doesn’t tell me to become someone new. It doesn’t demand transformation. It asks something more grounded: that I protect the perimeter of my life, that I let myself be helped in the work of restoration, and that I return carrying ordinary nourishment—proof that I can move through the world without shattering what matters.

If the previous night was about learning that my inner weather can be housed, this night is about learning that my house has an edge—and that my tenderness needs a fence, not to isolate me, but to give me a place where restoration can actually take hold.
