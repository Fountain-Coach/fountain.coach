Hotel Paris / 1947

I don’t enter this one like a dream. I drift into it the way a tired animal drifts into sunlight—carefully, almost suspiciously, and then, after a while, with trust. After so much attention given to my own workings—watching the watchman, mapping shock, measuring what I carry—my mind finds a quieter room to rest in. It is not an escape. It feels more like a controlled descent: a voluntary lowering of the nervous system into something sparse and breathable.

The space appears as an architectural thought.

A room sketched in clean lines, mostly empty, the way memory becomes when it stops trying to justify itself. On the left, a small dresser sits alone, modest and patient. The word PARIS is written nearby—not romantic, not postcard Paris, but Paris as a coordinate: a place-name pinned into the grid like a label on a drawer. Across the room, a bed lies low and simple, with the faintest suggestion of a body on it—more imprint than figure, as if I have been there, as if rest has already happened and left a pale residue.

Between these two—dresser and bed—stands a wall. Not a full wall: a slab, a partition, a vertical interruption. It has weight without aggression. And on that wall, calmly placed, is a year: 1947. A date that is not mine, and yet it enters as if it has always been waiting inside me. The number sits at mid-height like a watermark. A tiny orange dot clings to the date—my small heart-color, my marker for what matters—making the year feel touched, activated, chosen.

There’s a hairline running through the wall, a faint crack or seam, as if time itself has been scored there. I notice the crack the way I notice old grief: not as catastrophe, but as evidence. Proof that something endured pressure and did not disappear. The room does not dramatize it. It simply shows it.

What strikes me most is how little is required.

No crowd. No plot. No confrontation. Just a geometry of care: a place to put things away; a place to lie down; a partition that holds a date; a door that exists without demanding I walk through it. The door on the right stands open to an empty adjoining space—an invitation that doesn’t chase me. The kind of openness that finally feels safe: I can leave, but I don’t have to.

At the bottom, the little icon row appears like a legend for the body: small symbols—furniture, bed, door—then the stick-figure self with the orange dot. It’s as if the mind is saying: This is the inventory. This is enough to live. In this state, I don’t need to solve anything. I don’t need to interpret the date correctly. I don’t even need to remember why Paris. I only need to sit inside a scene that is held together by proportion and restraint.

And that is the sweetness of it: after so much inner labor, my mind allows itself a contemplative stillness that is not numbness. It is structured quiet. A resting in the framework. A private museum room where the exhibit is simply: time, place, and the permission to lie down.

Paris.
1947.
A small orange point that says: I am here, and I am not in danger.
