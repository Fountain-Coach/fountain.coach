# A Sound Must Earn Its Place

*On acoustic craftsmanship, machine-made music, and the difference between rendering and performing.*

**Status:** Complete editorial draft from the working conversation. Not yet author-approved, source-admitted, staged or publicly published. Do not silently rewrite or release it.

A sound can be generated correctly and still be entirely wrong.

It may contain the prescribed frequencies, follow the intended timing, respond to a controller, pass through an equalizer and arrive at the loudspeakers without a single technical error. Every measurement can be reproducible. The instrument has done what it was told to do.

And yet the result may sound cheap, lifeless, unconvincing. It may resemble something made for a demonstration rather than something that belongs in a recording, a film, a theatre or a room.

This is not necessarily a problem with the synthesis engine, the operating system or the digital audio format. It is a problem of artistic production. We have confused the ability to produce sound with the ability to make a sound worth hearing.

## The room after the film

The distinction became particularly apparent during the development of *Kino After Dark*, a small Csound Teatro interlude conceived around the illuminated façade of Berlin's Kino International.

The intention was an acoustic atmosphere: traces of cinema, remembered intimacy, the emotional residue of films, a room in which something had happened and whose silence still seemed occupied.

The first attempts were technically functional. Oscillators supplied bass notes; filtered noise produced percussion and air; a short repeating sample provided recognizable movement. MIDI 2.0 messages carried control information. Ear measured the signal and returned values that modulated an echo.

The machinery worked.

But none of those facts established that the listener had entered the imagined room.

A filtered noise generator does not become an atmospheric recording merely because we call its output film dust. A repeating phrase does not acquire the authority of a jazz performance by choosing approximately jazzy intervals. An echo cannot supply the emotional distance that the original sound has failed to establish.

Even a convincing interface can make the problem worse. The design appears finished while the acoustic work has barely begun.

The temptation is then to change technology. Move from Web Audio to native audio. Replace the browser with AUv3. Reduce latency, improve scheduling, expose more parameters and integrate a professional host.

Those may be useful engineering decisions. But they do not answer the artistic objection. A weak composition does not become strong because it is rendered by a native plug-in.

## The sound before the machine

In *The Door Is a Score*, we approached sound through the craft of the Geräuschemacher. The difference between an available recording and a dramatic acoustic performance depends on gesture, intention, perspective and time.

A door can close carefully, violently, reluctantly, or somewhere beyond the listener's view. The mechanical event is similar. The dramatic event is not.

This observation has consequences for synthetic sound.

A serious production begins by asking what the listener should encounter, not which oscillator or filter can be assigned to the task. What is the source? How does it behave? What gives it material identity? How is it excited? What remains audible after the principal event? What does its environment contribute? What changes when the listener moves away?

The source may be a recording of an actual instrument, a performed vocal gesture, a field recording, an elaborate physical model or an entirely synthetic construction. None is inherently superior. But each must be developed with sufficient material complexity, temporal articulation and expressive purpose.

A professional sound designer may spend considerable time finding a single characteristic noise, combining several performances, changing their perspective, removing an unwanted resonance and retaining an imperfection that makes the event credible.

The machine should be capable of supporting such work. It should not replace that work with a convenient approximation and call the approximation complete.

## What the ear can tell us

In *The Ear That Learns*, our synthetic singing instrument produced three performances of a short phrase. Ear measured differences between them. One articulation was selected as a useful development baseline.

That was a meaningful technical result. It was not evidence that the machine had learned to sing well.

The distinction is fundamental. Acoustic measurements can establish levels, spectral relationships, timing, distortion, onset characteristics and a wide range of other physical properties. They can detect problems and support comparisons. They can demonstrate that a processing operation had an effect.

They cannot, by themselves, determine whether a performance is moving, whether a timbre belongs in a composition, or whether the acoustic perspective supports the dramatic situation.

A recording with measurable distortion may be artistically indispensable. A technically clean recording may be lifeless. Perfect synchronization can destroy the quality of a human gesture. Irregularity is not automatically expression, just as precision is not automatically its enemy.

Ear must therefore remain an accountable observing instrument, not become a synthetic artistic director whose numerical preferences silently replace human listening.

The most useful machine listener is one that can identify what changed, locate where it changed, and help a human listener hear the consequence. It must also be able to acknowledge what its measurements cannot decide.

## A production standard, not a file specification

Fountain Coach needs to distinguish three kinds of acceptance.

Technical acceptance establishes that an instrument executes correctly. Its sources are identifiable, its timing and parameters are valid, its outputs can be reproduced, and its failures are handled safely.

Acoustic acceptance establishes that the produced signal meets the requirements of its intended medium. Source quality, noise, distortion, frequency balance, dynamic range, transient behaviour, stereo or spatial presentation and delivery processing must be examined under appropriate listening conditions. The canonical source should remain available in a suitable high-quality form; compressed distribution files must not silently become the production master.

Artistic acceptance establishes whether the sound belongs to the work.

That last decision cannot be replaced by the first two.

It requires listening to the actual sound at an appropriate level, on suitable monitoring equipment, in its dramatic and musical context. When references are used, comparisons should be level-matched and attentive to the qualities that make those recordings valuable: articulation, material, spatial depth, dynamics, phrasing and the relationships between performers.

A standard must make room for independent listeners and disagreement. A reference recording is not a recipe to be copied. Its purpose is to expose the quality of attention, production and performance that our own work must sustain.

Nor should artistic acceptance require conventional beauty. A harsh, narrow-band, damaged or deliberately crude sound may be exactly right. But its character should be a decision, not an unexplained limitation disguised as an aesthetic.

This is the difference between a sound that happens to be rough and one whose roughness has been composed.

## What an instrument must preserve

The promise of Csound Teatro is not that an artificial intelligence can manufacture endless sounds on demand.

Its more interesting possibility is that an authored acoustic composition can preserve the relationships through which sounds acquire meaning, while remaining open to performance, variation and correction.

A score can describe a source, a gesture, an acoustic perspective and a temporal relationship. Csound can realize an admitted sound process. MIDI 2.0 can communicate precise control. Ear can observe the resulting signal. Satzfunk can provide a listening and editorial context. Reframe can retain the work, its revisions and the relation between its source and its performances.

None of these instruments alone is responsible for the quality of the resulting art.

That responsibility belongs to the production as a whole, and ultimately to the people who listen, decide and authorize a work's release.

It follows that a generated sound must remain a candidate until it has been heard and judged. A successful render receipt cannot promote it into an artistic master. A passed browser test cannot qualify its musical expression. A technically correct processing chain cannot prove the acoustic credibility of the scene.

The instrument should make revision possible without concealing the original decision. Different takes, source recordings, processing choices, room responses and accepted editions must remain distinguishable.

This is how technical accountability can support artistic freedom rather than constrain it.

## The standard of attention

A cinematic sound environment might contain almost nothing: distant air, a low resonance, the remnant of a musical phrase, a movement that does not return.

Making that little sound convincing is demanding.

Its silence must have a character. The distance must be perceptible. The sound must reveal enough material identity to interest the listener without explaining itself. Its development must justify the time it occupies.

An artificial intelligence may contribute to every stage of that production. It may research, propose, synthesize, analyze, organize, compare and revise. But it should not mistake the abundance of generated possibilities for the quality of the eventual selection.

The question is not whether an instrument can produce something resembling cinema, jazz or a human voice.

The question is whether the resulting work can stand beside the acoustic art from which we have learned to listen.

That is a higher ambition than producing a convincing demonstration. It is also a more useful one.

Fountain Coach began with the idea that an instrument can extend human attention without claiming human judgment. Our audio practice must take that proposition seriously.

The technical system is responsible for doing what it declares. The production is responsible for the sound it makes. The artist is responsible for deciding what should be heard.

**A sound is not ready because the machine can make it. It is ready when the work can answer for it.**
