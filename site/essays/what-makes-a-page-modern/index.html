Was macht eine Seite modern?

Four settings, one text: the printed, spoken and addressable page—and what may still be changed.

An illustrated typesetter’s essay · Four settings · One source

WHAT MAKES A PAGE MODERN?01 / 12

The false answer

A page can look new and repeat an old decision.

Two pages lie on a table. One presents a centred title, a restrained serif, and paragraphs gathered into an even rectangle. The other puts the title to one side and lets the lines end where the words end. We call the first classical and the second modern. Yet neither name tells us what the page is for.

Before a printer chooses a face or an alignment, a more fundamental arrangement has begun. There is the Format, the outer dimension of the sheet. Within it lies the Satzspiegel—the type area in which words and images will be composed. Between that area and the cut edges are the Stege, the margins. The Zeilenfall determines where lines end; Durchschuss, the leading, gives them breathing room.

The useful question is not which page looks more up to date. It is which decisions its spaces make visible—and which activities those spaces permit.

WHAT MAKES A PAGE MODERN?04 / 12

A machine is not a style

Linotype and the New Typography are not synonyms.

A Linotype machine casts an assembled line of type as a single metal slug. It changed the labour, speed, and correction of printing. It did not prescribe asymmetry, a sans-serif, or a philosophy of empty space. A line may be cast by a machine and still occupy the most conventional of book pages.

The argument for a consciously modern page took another route. In Die neue Typographie (1928), Jan Tschichold defended clarity and functional organization against inherited habits of central alignment. The new arrangement treated paper as a field of relations. A headline might occupy one edge, a date another; information need not bow to a central axis.

But the classical page was never without intelligence. Its measure, rhythm, and margins could be extraordinarily exact. Tschichold himself later returned to classical book typography. The opposition is most useful as an experiment, not as a chronological verdict.

WHAT MAKES A PAGE MODERN?05 / 12

The proof comes back

The sheet becomes a place where writing continues.

In Dijon, Maurice Darantière’s printing house set the first edition of James Joyce’s Ulysses. The author received proofs and returned them with corrections, additions, and further passages. The work expanded during production. The printed surface had not ended composition; it had made revision newly visible.

Surviving placards were often large, single-sided working sheets, sometimes folded. They were not contemporary A4 handouts, and we should not counterfeit their appearance. What is worth recovering is the relation between the gesetzt—the matter already set—and the margin available for what has not yet found its place.

A proof can therefore be read in two directions: inward, through the precise lines the printer has already made; outward, toward the still uncomposed work. The Außensteg is no longer just the border of a book. It becomes an instrument.

WHAT MAKES A PAGE MODERN?07 / 12

The text remembers

The third setting can be returned to its source.

At the edge of the screen a cursor moves with the spoken word. It can be attached to a passage and a character position before that passage is ever printed. A MIDI 2.0 position message may follow its movement, but the message itself is a number. The literary identity belongs to the admitted semantic unit and its source address—not to a MIDI note or a page number.

When the same unit crosses a physical sheet, or is cut away and pasted elsewhere, its origin should remain recoverable. The scanner’s first duty is modest: to observe the material, compare it with the known setting, and preserve what cannot be decided. An arrow in the margin may instruct, question, compare, or simply occupy the paper. It is not automatically a command.

The writer must decide what becomes a new revision. The compositor then sets again. Modernity, in this sense, is not an appearance; it is the ability to change the setting without losing the history of the work.

WHAT MAKES A PAGE MODERN?08 / 12

A page without one size

The reader changes its measure, not its source.

On paper, the Satzspiegel holds still long enough for a pencil to enter it. On a screen the rectangle is less obedient. Turn an iPad, enlarge the type, or narrow a window, and a sentence will fall into different lines. The folio is a consequence of the setting. It cannot be the address of the sentence.

What persists is the admitted source: the work, its revision, and the semantic unit to which a passage belongs. Its identity must remain available whether the text is printed in an A4 proof, composed into a two-page spread, or read on a narrow screen. A different Zeilenfall need not create a different work.

The web reader should therefore resist the temptation to mimic a fixed PDF. It is a further composition of the book. Its page is permitted to change; its testimony is not.

WHAT MAKES A PAGE MODERN?09 / 12

When the word moves

The cursor joins two coordinates without confusing them.

A voice makes a passage happen in time. A page gives the same passage a position in space. They are not interchangeable. A spoken moment has an audio time and a corresponding interval of characters in an admitted source. The moving cursor is their temporary meeting.

In our web reader this should remain almost invisible as an apparatus. There is one text, one spoken reading, one page composition. Play and pause belong at the scale of the page, not in a dashboard. Following the voice can be the quiet default. The passage is marked where it already sits; a second transcript or an endless scrolling copy would divide the work against itself.

When an iPad turns or a paragraph breaks across two leaves, the old rectangle disappears. The cursor must find the word again by its source interval, not by remembering where yesterday’s pixels stood. The narrator supplies time; the compositor supplies space; neither becomes the author.

WHAT MAKES A PAGE MODERN?10 / 12

A number is not a name

Two parts of a position event have different duties.

The cursor can also speak to instruments. In a bounded Fountain Press prototype a MIDI 2.0 Control Change message—CC 20 in the book’s own convention—represents a normalized position. A source-address record alongside the browser event names the document, revision, digest, semantic unit and character interval. One part is numerical movement; the other says which writing that movement concerns.

They must not be collapsed. A Universal MIDI Packet does not magically contain an arbitrary literary identifier, and a controller number is not a sentence. Once a message leaves the browser for another MIDI 2.0 device, the receiver needs an admitted mapping or separate identity contract. Otherwise the machine receives a position without knowing its work.

This distinction opens artistic possibilities rather than restricting them. A passage may cue sound or a Teatro scene while remaining exactly the passage the printer set. Performance can address the text. It does not own it.

WHAT MAKES A PAGE MODERN?11 / 12

The page can answer back

The outer margin is a physical interface.

The fourth setting leads us back to the third. The reader has followed a passage on screen, perhaps heard its reading and followed the corresponding position event. Now the printer sets the same source on A4, but with a narrow Satzspiegel and an unusually generous Außensteg. The empty region is for another kind of action.

A word is crossed out. A line is continued in pencil. A paragraph is cut away and placed beside another. When the altered sheet returns as a photograph, a semantic scan can begin from the known edition and its measured fragments. It should record what is visible, propose a correspondence, and preserve what remains uncertain. The pencil stroke does not become a command merely because a machine can see it.

The voice, the page, and the MIDI event are not competing editions. They are different ways of approaching one source. An author decides whether any intervention becomes a revision; only then does the compositor set another proof. What travels between those settings is neither a pixel nor a note, but accountable identity.

AN EDITORIAL COLOPHON13

From sheet to reader

Von der Druckfahne zur Web-Ausgabe

A printed sheet has a fixed measure. This reader has another: its lines must be set again when the window changes. Neither rendering may change the words of the admitted work. The digital edition does not replace the printed proof; it makes another reading possible while preserving the source to which future corrections must return.

Distribution adds obligations rather than a new author. The edition identifies its source, revision, publisher and reading state; a page number is never mistaken for the identity of a passage. An unfinished MIDI 2.0 instrument is not presented as performed music, and an absent spoken take is not represented by a pretend Play control.

What the physical proof invites us to add in pencil, the web edition asks us to preserve as evidence. Only a subsequent editorial decision may alter the work. The page remains complete only provisionally.
