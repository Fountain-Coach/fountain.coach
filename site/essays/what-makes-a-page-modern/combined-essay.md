# What Makes a Page Modern?
The printed, spoken and addressable page — and Self Out Loud

## The false answer

Two pages lie on a table. One presents a centred title, a restrained serif, and paragraphs gathered into an even rectangle. The other puts the title to one side and lets the lines end where the words end. We call the first classical and the second modern. Yet neither name tells us what the page is for.

Before a printer chooses a face or an alignment, a more fundamental arrangement has begun. There is the Format, the outer dimension of the sheet. Within it lies the Satzspiegel—the type area in which words and images will be composed. Between that area and the cut edges are the Stege, the margins. The Zeilenfall determines where lines end; Durchschuss, the leading, gives them breathing room.

The useful question is not which page looks more up to date. It is which decisions its spaces make visible—and which activities those spaces permit.

A page can look new and repeat an old decision. It can also look entirely conventional while allowing something genuinely new to happen.

The next two settings form a small demonstration within the page: two distinctly framed type specimens of one passage. Their words are deliberately identical; their composition changes.

## The first setting

In the first specimen, a centred heading and even measure place the passage inside a familiar book tradition. This is the first setting of the demonstration, not another paragraph of the essay.

Neither arrangement is inherently more intelligent. The same words acquire another pace, not another author.

## A machine is not a style

A Linotype machine casts an assembled line of type as a single metal slug. It changed the labour, speed and correction of printing. It did not prescribe asymmetry, a sans-serif face or a philosophy of empty space. A line could be cast by a machine and still occupy the most conventional of book pages.

The argument for a consciously modern page took another route. In Die neue Typographie (1928), Jan Tschichold defended clarity and functional organization against inherited habits of central alignment. The new arrangement treated paper as a field of relations. A headline might occupy one edge, a date another; information need not bow to a central axis.

But classical typography was never without intelligence. Its measure, rhythm and margins could be extraordinarily exact. Tschichold himself later returned to classical book typography.

The opposition is therefore most useful as an experiment, not as a chronological verdict. It shows how a change of arrangement can change the experience of reading while leaving the written source intact.

And it prepares a further question. What happens when the page is no longer merely something to be read?

## The proof comes back

In Dijon, Maurice Darantière's printing house set the first edition of James Joyce's Ulysses. The author received proofs and returned them with corrections, additions and further passages. The work expanded during production.

The printed surface had not ended composition. It had made revision newly visible.

Surviving placards were often large, single-sided working sheets, sometimes folded. They were not contemporary A4 handouts, and there is little value in counterfeiting their appearance. What is worth recovering is the relation between the gesetzt—the matter already set—and the margin available for what has not yet found its place.

A proof can be read in two directions: inward, through the precise lines the printer has already made; outward, toward the still uncomposed work.

The Außensteg is no longer just the border of a book. It becomes an instrument.

## The third setting

A word is crossed out. A line is continued. Another sentence occupies the space beside the first. The printed setting provides a stable object against which a change can be considered.

Yet the pencil mark is not the revised manuscript. It is the material trace of an intervention. Someone must still decide what the next setting will contain.

The modern page inherits this distinction between what has been composed, what has been observed and what has been authorized to change.

[[plate:proof]]

## A page without one size

On paper, the Satzspiegel holds still long enough for a pencil to enter it. On a screen, the rectangle is less obedient.

Turn an iPad, enlarge the type or narrow a window, and a sentence will fall into different lines. A paragraph may move from one leaf to another. Two pages may become one. The folio is a consequence of the setting; it cannot be the enduring address of the sentence.

What persists is the written source: the work, its revision and the semantic unit to which a passage belongs. Its identity must remain available whether the text is printed as a proof, composed into a two-page spread or read on a narrow screen.

A different Zeilenfall need not create a different work.

The digital reader should therefore resist the temptation to imitate a fixed PDF. It is a further composition of the book. Its page is permitted to change; its testimony is not.

[[plate:reader]]

This makes a different relationship with the text possible. A passage can be found again after the screen rotates. An annotation can remain attached to the sentence even when that sentence moves. A spoken reading can continue while the page is recomposed beneath it.

The position of the writing and the position of the reader are no longer identical. To connect them, we need another kind of address.

[[plate:address]]

## When the word moves

A voice makes a passage happen in time. A page gives the same passage a position in space. They are not interchangeable.

A spoken moment has an audio time and, when its relationship with the text has been established, a corresponding interval of characters in the source. A moving cursor can become their temporary meeting.

This has already become familiar in synthetic readings. A voice speaks an authored passage, its recording is aligned with the source, and the reading position follows the sound. The appropriate word is marked where it already sits on the page. When the reading reaches the end of a leaf, the page can turn.

The apparatus need hardly appear. A small Play or Pause action belongs at the scale of the page, not in a dashboard. There is no need for a second transcript or an endlessly scrolling copy of the text. The page itself is the transcript.

When the screen changes orientation, the old word rectangle disappears. The cursor must find the word again by its source address, not by remembering yesterday's pixels.

[[plate:cursor]]

## A number is not a name

This relationship can also be communicated to instruments. MIDI 2.0 allows control events to carry finely resolved musical and temporal information. In a reading instrument, a controller can represent movement through a passage. A separate source-address record establishes which work, revision and character interval that movement concerns.

The two must not be confused. A number is not a sentence. A MIDI message does not magically contain literary meaning.

But the distinction opens a possibility. A passage may direct the movement of a page, cue a sound or become part of a dramatic composition while remaining identifiable as the writing from which the event arose.

The voice can begin to operate the page.

So far, the most convenient example has been the synthetic voice. It begins with the known text. Once its recording has been checked and aligned, a predictable relationship can be established between the written passage and the spoken event.

But the writer has a voice, too.

[[plate:midi]]

## The writer is the first actor

A writer reads a sentence aloud and discovers that something is wrong. The words are all present. The grammar may be correct. Nothing on the page necessarily indicates a problem. Yet the sentence resists being spoken.

Perhaps it asks for a breath where no breath belongs. Perhaps its emphasis arrives too late. A thought that appeared complete turns out to contain another thought, still waiting for its words. The writer reads again.

Actors know that a written sentence is not yet a performed sentence. Its punctuation offers indications, but its duration, weight and direction remain undecided. A pause may confirm a thought or put it into question. Emphasis may transform a statement into an accusation, an invitation or a joke.

The actor discovers these possibilities by speaking. A writer reading aloud encounters a related freedom, except that the text itself may still be changing.

This does not require a trained actor's voice. Nor must the reading be fluent, beautiful or intended for an audience. Hesitation, repetition and the unfinished phrase may be precisely what makes the exercise useful.

The writer is the first actor of the work, even when no public performance is intended. Reading aloud is not simply a way of reproducing writing. It is one of the ways writing can continue.

What would happen if the page followed this voice with the same care it can already give an aligned synthetic reading?

## Self Out Loud

Imagine opening a manuscript and beginning to read.

The page follows the spoken passage. Its cursor moves with the words. When the end of the leaf is reached, the page turns. When the writer pauses, it waits.

There is no need to touch the screen merely to preserve the reading position.

The writer may return to a sentence, begin again or change its emphasis. The page should accommodate these ordinary departures without treating them as failures.

This is the basic idea of Self Out Loud: to make the writer's own voice capable of operating the modern page.

It requires a different kind of intelligence from the one that produces synthetic speech. The machine can generate a fluent reading because it already knows the words it intends to say. The writer, however, may not yet know what the next sentence ought to be.

A phrase may be repeated. An unfamiliar name may interrupt the rhythm. An unexpected association may lead away from the manuscript. A word may be pronounced differently from the form the system anticipates.

The page must not require the writer to become more predictable in order to remain useful.

If recognition becomes uncertain, the cursor should retain its last trustworthy position rather than wander through the text. The instrument may use the surrounding passage to recover its place. It can offer a possible correspondence without pretending to certainty.

And when the relationship becomes clear again, the reading can continue. This is not a demand for perfect recognition. It is a demand for graceful assistance.

## If a word becomes difficult

Pronunciation reveals the difference particularly well.

A writer encounters a foreign name, an unfamiliar expression or a sequence of sounds that refuses to come easily. The page already contains the written word. It can use that knowledge to assist the speaker.

Perhaps the word is divided into syllables. Perhaps stress is indicated. Perhaps a short example can be heard. The writer listens, tries again or simply continues.

Nothing in this exchange requires the page to act as an examiner.

An accent is not an error merely because it differs from a model's preferred pronunciation. A poet may deliberately disturb the usual rhythm of a word. An actor may choose an unusual articulation because it changes the relationship between characters. A writer may discover that an apparently mistaken pronunciation belongs to the work.

The system should distinguish between uncertainty in its own recognition and an error it has no authority to declare. The useful question is not whether the writer has performed correctly, but whether the instrument can help the writer continue.

This lowers the threshold of the entire reading mechanism. The carefully aligned synthetic voice has demonstrated the relationship between word, time, position and page turn. Self Out Loud asks that the same capabilities become available to a human speaker without requiring synthetic precision.

The machinery grows more accommodating so that the writer can remain less concerned with it.

## The voice becomes an instrument

Once a voice can guide the reading position, other possibilities become apparent.

A voice is not merely a sequence of recognized words. It contains breath, intensity, pitch, rhythm, articulation, silence and changes of intention.

These qualities can enter relationships with musical and dramatic instruments. MIDI 2.0 offers a means of communicating expressive controls. A vocal gesture might influence a sound field. Breath might become rhythm. Speech might become melody, or begin a transformation that no longer resembles speech at all.

There is no reason to decide these outcomes in advance.

An instrument acquires meaning through what people discover by using it. Its first responsibility is not to demonstrate every possible transformation but to remain available for an action that matters.

For the writer, the first action may be no more complicated than reading a paragraph while the page follows. The same voice may later become material for a composition, a dramatic rehearsal or an entirely unexpected form.

The recording itself can remain available, distinct from the controls and transformations derived from it. The written sentence retains its identity. The MIDI event retains its timing and technical meaning. Their relationship can be examined without pretending that one is a complete representation of the other.

A performance can address the work without replacing its words. And a writer can discover a performance without having intended to produce one.

## The returned voice

The printer's proof gives us another way to understand what happens next. A writer records a passage and listens again. The recording may reveal a repeated word, a hesitation, an improvised phrase or a silence that has become important.

These events can be associated with positions in the manuscript. Recognition can propose differences between what was written and what was spoken. An annotation may occupy the corresponding margin.

But a spoken departure is not automatically a correction.

A hesitation is not necessarily a missing comma. A repetition is not necessarily a mistake. An improvised sentence may be a discovery, a discarded possibility or simply something that happened in the moment.

As with a pencil mark on a returned proof, the first duty is to preserve the evidence. A writer may decide to change the text. The compositor can then set another page. Alternatively, the writer may retain the original sentence and choose the recorded reading as a separate interpretation.

The possibilities need not compete.

What travels between the written passage, the recording, the MIDI event and the next composition is neither a pixel nor a note, but an accountable relationship.

The work can continue without losing its history.

## Conditions of composition

The classical page, the modernist arrangement, the returned printer's proof and the digital reader are different settings of a related problem. They ask how writing may remain legible while still being available for change.

Six conditions emerge.

01  The source survives the setting. Das Werk bleibt von seinem Satz unterscheidbar.

02  The measure is declared, not guessed. Format, Satzspiegel und Durchschuss werden benannt.

03  White space is available for work. Der freie Raum ist kein Rest.

04  A mark is evidence before it is an instruction. Eine Anmerkung ist noch keine Änderung.

05  A position event is not a source identity. Ein Positionswert ist keine Textidentität.

06  Only the writer authorizes the next composition. Die nächste Fassung braucht eine Entscheidung.

Self Out Loud does not require another principle. It puts these six to work through the speaking body. The source remains the writing. The page retains its measure. Its margin is available for intervention. The voice produces events that can be observed without automatically becoming revisions. MIDI 2.0 communicates positions and expressive controls without confusing them with literary identity.

And the writer decides what follows.

## What makes the page modern?

We began with two pages on a table. One was classical, the other modern. Their differences were visible in typography, alignment and the organization of empty space. Neither could answer our question.

The printer's proof brought us closer. It showed that a page might be precisely composed without being finished, that its margins could belong to the continuation of writing.

The digital page adds a further possibility. Its composition can change while the identity of its text survives. Its reading position can be connected to spoken time. Its controls need not remain exclusively under the fingers of the reader.

And now the writer can speak.

A sentence begins. The cursor follows. A difficult word receives assistance. The voice hesitates, the page waits, and the writer finds the next phrase.

Perhaps the sentence changes. Perhaps the writer discovers that its existing rhythm is right. Perhaps the voice begins something musical that was not previously imagined.

The page does not decide.

Its modernity lies neither in the appearance of its type nor in the sophistication of its machinery. It lies in the possibilities the composition leaves available to the person working with it.

The classical page gives the writing a measure. The proof leaves room for the hand. The addressable page preserves the source through its changing forms. Self Out Loud lets the page follow the writer's voice.

The page is complete only provisionally. The work may continue.
