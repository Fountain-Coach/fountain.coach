FCIS, canonical skills and the prospect of a first sale

Fountain Coach has built a technological asset. Whether that asset can become a business depends on a different kind of proof.

A different kind of possession

What we possess is not an answer that an AI once gave. It is a means of making a requested action accountable.

Fountain Coach's FCIS work combines authored agent procedures with software contracts, permission boundaries, source history and records of verification. Each of these is familiar in isolation. Their relationship is more unusual: a human purpose is translated into a bounded operation without allowing the agent's description of success to stand in for the event itself. A persuasive account is not a receipt.

There is genuine material here. The organization maintains canonical skill sources in Git, original implementations of its host and instruments, validation routines, publishing procedures and an authored body of governance. These can be maintained, improved, licensed or operated as a service, subject to the rights attached to particular components. The accumulation of tested decisions also matters: it is knowledge that would take work to reconstruct.

But ownership must be drawn precisely. Fountain Coach does not own the models that may read a skill, nor Git, MCP, OAuth, WebAuthn, MIDI 2.0 or Stripe. Nor does control of a repository by itself prove exclusive commercial title to every contribution and dependency within it. A public repository is not necessarily an open commercial license; the actual licenses and contributor rights must be checked before redistributing anything.

Our defensible possession is therefore narrower, and stronger, than a claim to own an AI platform in general. We control a particular arrangement of authored procedures, executable capabilities, evidence standards and operational experience. That arrangement can be offered to customers only insofar as its individual rights and actual behavior are established.

A skill is not yet a service

The current canonical catalogue contains six skills. Teatro Scenario Composer addresses musical and Csound work; Linotype Estate Template addresses faithful book composition; FCIS Skill Authoring and Sync governs the source and distribution of procedures. FCIS Owner Access describes passkey-first sign-in and recovery. EstatePublisher Deployment governs release practice, while Satzfunk Voice Render concerns source-bound narration. Together they cover several demanding kinds of work, but they are not six interchangeable products on a shelf.

A skill directs an agent. It does not create the runtime instrument, supply the operating authority, own an external provider's service, or certify that a requested effect has occurred. In FCIS the distinction is deliberate. The procedure belongs to canonical source; the typed capability belongs to its implementation; the permission belongs to the proper authorization system; and execution evidence must come from the operation actually performed.

This division may be commercially important. Organizations already have AI systems capable of writing plausible plans. The difficult question is whether those systems can be allowed to change something consequential without confusing intention, permission, action and proof. FCIS offers a design for holding these apart. It is a potential advantage, not yet evidence of a market preference.

Where the evidence ends

The recorded state on 12 November 2026 is unusually specific. The six canonical packages were projected into one Git-backed plugin; the source record reports 144 passing regression tests and version 0.1.15. The workspace marketplace displayed all six skill cards after synchronization. Those are meaningful checks of source governance and distribution.

They are not a substitute for independent proof of every hosted package byte or of the skill selected in a fresh session. Those acceptance gates remain open in the current project record. And the newly admitted owner-access procedure explicitly does not mean that passkey login and recovery have already been implemented. The existing Host has demonstrated a bounded host.describe operation, but one successful operation does not establish a multi-tenant commercial product.

This is not a defect in the assessment; it is the assessment's necessary limit. If FCIS has value because it separates claims from observations, an account of FCIS must obey the same rule. Source-tested, hosted, live-accepted and commercially operated are different states. We should present each in its proper tense.

Why worth is a difficult number

To ask what FCIS is worth may mean three distinct things. One may ask what it would cost to rebuild the source and hard-won working knowledge. One may ask what rights an acquirer could obtain in the code, procedures and associated methods. Or one may ask what future customers would pay for the results. These questions have different answers and require different evidence.

The first points to replacement effort, and that effort is substantial in kind even where it has not been formally costed. The second depends on a rights inventory: original authorship, repository history, dependency licenses, contractor or contributor terms, and the precise assets included in any transaction. The third is a market test. Tests and source control can substantiate technical maturity; neither can create a customer.

That is why a headline valuation in euros would be premature. There is no defensible route from six skills or 144 passing tests to a six-figure or seven-figure enterprise price. Capital spent developing an asset is not the same as a price someone will pay to acquire it, and neither is identical to the cash the business might earn.

The more useful question is smaller: which outcome would someone outside Fountain Coach purchase twice? A repeated purchase begins to tell us whether our methods are not only original, but economically necessary to someone else.

The first purchasable outcome

Stripe could charge a customer for access to a Fountain Coach service. It would provide checkout, subscription administration and payment events, not the technical authority to execute an instrument. A successful charge may establish a commercial entitlement. The existing FCIS authorization path must still decide who may act, on what resource, under which limits and with which revocation rights.

The initial offer should not be a general subscription to an abstract intelligence infrastructure. A customer should buy a comprehensible outcome. Verified Publishing is a credible candidate because Fountain Coach already knows how to work with source-bound editions, controlled releases and readback evidence. The offer might be to take an authorized manuscript or edition, compose it faithfully, release it under a defined contract and return both the publication and an inspectable account of what was done.

That would remain a proposed product until the complete customer path has been accepted. In particular, customer isolation, rights to submitted material, service limits, billing-event handling, failure and refund behavior, support obligations, and the purchase-to-result-to-revocation sequence need direct tests. We should not invent a second permission system to receive payments; we should connect billing to the existing governed authority, with the smallest real adaptation the customer path requires.

One invoice, one witnessed result

A hypothetical price of €1,500 to set up an organization and €390 per month might help frame a pilot conversation. It is not a researched market rate. Twenty customers paying the monthly figure would amount to €7,800 per month, or €93,600 a year, in gross recurring subscription charges. Hosting, model use, payment fees, sales, support, taxes and the founder's own working time would all reduce what remains. Nor does a multiplication establish that twenty willing customers exist.

For that reason the first meaningful commercial milestone is not a revenue projection. It is one external customer who pays for a tightly defined service, invokes an authorized FCIS-backed operation, receives the promised result, and can independently inspect the evidence. A second successful purchase would matter even more: it would begin to separate an exceptional demonstration from a business capable of repetition.

FCIS may eventually be licensed as a governed agent infrastructure for other organizations. That is an intelligible long-term possibility. The near-term proposition is simpler. Sell one reliable outcome, learn the real cost of delivering it, and find out whether customers regard the distinction between plausible AI output and witnessed work as worth paying for.
