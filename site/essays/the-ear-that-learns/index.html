The Ear That Learns

On Sing, Ear, and the old discipline of learning through a voice.

A note may be perfectly in tune and still fail to become a word. A voice may produce sound without having learned to sing. And a machine may measure every frequency in a recording without yet knowing what it has heard.

For a while, the development of Sing could be described in the language of construction. We built a native voice, gave it phonetic states, connected its musical events to MIDI 2.0, and verified that its resonators generated a phrase. Then the more difficult question arrived. Could another instrument, listening independently, recognize the syllables?

The phrase was almost comically small: la mi na. Three syllables, four notes. The first vowel survived a change of pitch; a consonant did not have to begin again merely because the melody moved. Breath and pressure could alter a line. The resulting recordings could be reconstructed and compared by their exact digital identities. These were substantial achievements. They were not yet evidence that someone had sung intelligible words.

A note is not a syllable

Sound production is remarkably generous with false encouragement. A waveform looks persuasive. An autocorrelation curve can reveal the intended note. A spectral comparison can distinguish a sustained vowel from a nasal onset. The articulated version of our little phrase gave Ear a better acoustic starting point than the plain version. But acoustic improvement is not the same thing as language.

We tried another route, beginning with a naturalistic speaking voice and turning speech toward sustained, pitched sound. Some versions preserved recognizable fragments; others lost them when the spectrum moved. An apparently better vowel could make a word worse. The score had remained stable; the heard result had not. This is why an instrument should not be allowed to examine its own output and award itself success.

The two instruments acquire their identities through this disagreement. Sing is the performer in development. Ear is the listening boundary. Neither owns the writer's text, a teacher's authority, the rights in a recording, or the editorial decision to publish. The source, score, phonetic plan, voice and recording remain distinguishable objects, even when an audience eventually hears them as one event.

What the listening machine knows

On 25 October 2026, Ear acquired another kind of witness: a real Japanese singing reference from the PJS corpus, accompanied by a separately produced human revision of its phoneme timings. The revision preserved the original recording. What changed was the account of where the sounds began and ended. The original annotation identified 71 non-pause phonemes; the revised annotation identified 73. Matched phoneme boundaries were often shifted by roughly a quarter of a second.

This does not establish that one annotator was right and the other wrong. It establishes something more useful: a reference is also an interpretation. Even a carefully labelled recording does not release a listener from having to examine how the labels were made.

Ear's experimental phoneme recognizer was configured for English. The Japanese labels did not become English labels simply because both could be represented as strings. The correct response was therefore to withhold the result. Vocal activity had been detected in the Japanese performance, but Japanese phonemes had not been independently recognized. No qualified singer had yet been established.

There is an ethical quality to this small technical refusal. A listener who hears something uncertain should be able to say I do not know. In an educational setting, that admission is more valuable than an answer manufactured to agree with the exercise sheet.

The old European lessons

What is a singer taught to hear? European classical training offers several answers, not one recipe. In Italian bel canto, a vowel is a place where a tone can continue living. Consonants must arrive without unnecessarily breaking the line; a long note is not simply a syllable stretched by arithmetic. A swell and withdrawal of sound, a messa di voce, teaches the ear how a voice may change intensity while retaining its center.

German Lied and operatic declamation ask for a related but different attention. The word has an argument before it has a melody. A consonant cluster may carry urgency; the distance between spoken emphasis and musical accent can become the drama. Pronouncing Licht is not only selecting the correct phonemes. It is placing an audible word inside a phrase that means something.

Spanish lyric theatre supplies another valuable threshold. In zarzuela, speaking and singing may alternate within the same dramatic encounter. A performer does not become a different person whenever the accompaniment begins. Accent, breath, pacing and intention cross that boundary. If Sing is ever to move convincingly from speaking to singing, it must preserve more than a dictionary pronunciation: the relation between a spoken gesture and a musical one.

None of these traditions is a language preset. Italian opera, German Lied and Spanish zarzuela belong to histories of teachers, performers, rooms, audiences and particular works. Their methods can inform exercises, but no single acoustic measure can certify style or artistry.

Before the drum is struck

Then there is tabla, where the teaching begins with a wonderful reversal. The student may learn to speak the rhythm before playing it. Syllables called bols name and organize strokes; a cycle of beats can be marked with the hands. The musical pattern is heard, spoken, remembered, imitated, varied, and only then returned to the drum. Recitation is not a poor substitute for performance. It is one way musical knowledge becomes available to the body.

For Ear and Sing, this is not an invitation to translate tabla syllables into operatic phonemes. Tabla recitation has its own rhythmic and practical functions, its own teachers and repertoires. The lesson to borrow is structural: an audible demonstration can become a symbolic memory, a repeated action, a correction and a new attempt without losing its place in a larger cycle.

Hindustani vocal practice deserves its own place beside this example. A sustained drone establishes a listening relation; swara and sargam exercises cultivate the ear's sense of pitch, movement and return. Daily riyaz is not simply the repetition of identical files. It is a practice in which listening and producing become increasingly difficult to separate.

Both approaches correct a computational fantasy: that musical understanding begins with a large enough archive. A tradition may instead begin with a teacher's small phrase and the student's imperfect answer.

Correction is part of the composition

The next development is therefore not merely the acquisition of more recordings. We need a rehearsal whose relations can be inspected. A teacher demonstrates. A learner attempts. Ear listens without seeing the answer sheet. A comparison identifies what can be established and what remains uncertain. One adjustment is proposed; the phrase is attempted again. Later, an unfamiliar phrase and a different performer test whether anything learned has travelled beyond the original exercise.

Such a loop is deceptively difficult to implement. Teaching material may show a student the intended words, but an independent examiner must not secretly receive them before recognition. Rhythmic error is not phonetic error. A stable note is not good diction. An acceptable transcription is not an artistic interpretation, nor an authorization to imitate someone's voice. A useful system must preserve these differences when it would be easier to collapse them.

We have now written Sing's first teaching contract: separate exercises for Italian, German and Spanish vocal work, tabla bol recitation, and Hindustani vocal practice. Their structure has passed local tests. They contain no borrowed recordings, no trained teacher and no claimed recognition result. The contract is a score for future investigation, not a performance pretending to have occurred.

The next sound

There is a certain modesty in the name Ear. It does not promise understanding. It names the organ by which a difference may first become perceptible. Sing names an action, not a completed virtuosity. Between the two lies the space occupied by teachers, students, instruments and all the awkward repetitions through which something becomes possible.

We began with four notes and three syllables. We have not yet built a qualified singer, and the Japanese phoneme-recognition test remains open. But we have found a more interesting engineering question than whether a machine can generate a convincing sound: can it enter a discipline in which listening changes the next attempt?

The next sound need not be a perfect one. It must be one whose correction can be heard.

Essay and evidence, kept distinct. This is authored interpretation of ongoing research, not a claim of intelligible synthetic singing or a completed teacher. The Ear annotation report, Sing teaching contract and open work retain technical evidence. Meet Ear and Sing as instruments.
